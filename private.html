Private by design

The private retirement planner

Most retirement calculators ask you to open an account and hand over your finances. This one does not, and cannot — it is a single file that runs on your own computer, with the internet switched off if you like.

Why most retirement calculators want an account

Not usually for your benefit. An account lets a planner store your figures on its servers, which is what makes a subscription enforceable, a mailing list possible and a company valuation larger. It also means your income, savings, ages and health assumptions sit on someone else’s computer — subject to their security, their policy changes, their acquisition, and their pricing.

That trade is worth making for some products. For a calculator that does arithmetic on numbers you already know, it is worth asking what you are getting in return.

What “private” means here, precisely

The AI Retirement Income Planner is one self-contained HTML file. You download it, open it, and everything happens inside your own browser:

  • No account and no sign-up. There is no login, so there is no profile, no password to leak and nothing to cancel.
  • No uploads. Your figures live in your own browser’s storage. There is no server that receives them, because there is no server.
  • No bank connections. The planner never links to a financial account.
  • No analytics inside the product. No telemetry, no crash reporting, no usage tracking of any kind.
  • It works offline. Opening the file makes no network request at all — there are no external scripts, fonts or stylesheets in it.

You can verify this in about ten seconds

This is the part that separates a privacy claim from a privacy policy. Open the planner, press F12 to bring up your browser’s developer tools, and watch the Network tab while you build an entire plan. You will see exactly one request: a look-up of published currency conversion rates, which carries nothing about you. Not one figure from your plan leaves.

If that sounds fiddly, there is a blunter version: disconnect from the internet and keep working. Every number still runs. A planner that needs an account cannot pass either test, no matter how reassuring its privacy page reads.

Every request the planner is capable of making is listed here — there are only a handful, and none of them carry your plan.

The one exception, in our own words

The planner has an optional AI co-pilot. If you switch it on and choose a cloud provider — Anthropic, OpenAI, Google or OpenRouter — a summary of your plan is sent to that provider so it can answer, using an API key you supply and pay for. It never passes through us and we never see it, but it does leave your computer.

Three things follow: the co-pilot is off until you turn it on, the planner is fully functional without it, and if you want AI and complete privacy you can point it at a local model running on your own machine, which keeps everything offline.

Who this tends to matter to

People who are self-employed or hold company equity, and would rather their compensation not sit in a marketing database. People planning a move abroad, whose residency and tax position is nobody else’s business. People who have simply been breached before. And people who object on principle to renting access to their own arithmetic.

If none of that is you, the planner is worth having on the modelling alone — the privacy is just how it happens to be built.

What you actually get

A month-by-month retirement income engine covering five phases, US, UK, Canadian and Australian tax treatment, Social Security timing, ACA and Medicare thresholds, Roth conversions, Monte Carlo and historical backtesting. Bought once, owned outright, with no subscription and no renewal.
