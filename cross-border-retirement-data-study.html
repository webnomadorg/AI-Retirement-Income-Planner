Original research

The same $1.2M retirement, modeled in four countries

We took one retirement plan and ran it, month by month to age 90, as a tax resident of the United States, United Kingdom, Canada and Australia. Same savings, same withdrawals, same Social Security — only the tax system and the healthcare bill change.

Download the full dataset → — free, CC BY 4.0.

Re-run on 6 October 2026 with the current planner. The first edition (August 2026) put the UK ahead at every age. The planner it ran on taxed a UK resident's US Social Security in the US instead of the UK; we corrected that in September, and the UK's lead now ends at 65. Every figure on this page is from the re-run.

Three things the numbers say

The order changes

The country that is best the year you retire is not the one that is best at 80. In the base case the UK leads from 60 to 65. From 65 the United States does, and at 67 Canada and Australia swap places, without any of them doing anything differently.

At 67, Social Security moves the tax

Under the US–UK treaty, US Social Security is taxed only where you live. For the UK resident the whole benefit enters the UK tax base, and their bill rises to more than twice the American's. Free UK healthcare no longer covers the gap.

It depends how much you have

The UK starts ahead at every size up to $1.8M; at $2.5M the United States does. By 80 the United States leads at every size from $900k up. Only at $600k does the UK stay in front to the end.

The plan we modeled

A single filer retiring at 60 with $1,200,000 — $800,000 in a 401(k), $300,000 in a taxable brokerage account and $100,000 in cash — drawing $7,000 a month across the three, and claiming $2,800/month of Social Security at 67. Once Social Security starts, portfolio withdrawals are trimmed by what it replaces, so total income stays level rather than stepping up.

The plan is then run four times. The only thing that changes is where the person is tax resident. Phase boundaries land on the events that actually change the arithmetic: Medicare at 65, the Social Security claim at 67, Required Minimum Distributions at 73, and 80.

Why the order changes

The UK's lead at 60 comes from healthcare. What ends it is tax, and the tax that changes is on Social Security.

Before 65, the UK resident pays more income tax than the American and still comes out ahead. $607 against $353 a month, a gap of $254. The American is also buying insurance on the ACA marketplace at $456 a month, and the UK resident is not.

From 65 to 67 it is close to a tie. Medicare costs the American $291 a month, less than the marketplace did, and the UK resident's tax gap has grown to $299. The United States edges ahead by $8.

From 67, Social Security decides it. Under the US–UK treaty (Article 17(3)), US Social Security paid to a UK resident is taxable only in the UK, so the whole benefit enters the UK tax base. In the US, at most 85% of it is taxable, and the standard and senior deductions shelter much of that. The UK bill rises to $850 a month against the American's $343. The 401(k) withdrawals are taxed in both countries, but the Foreign Tax Credit cancels the US share, so the UK resident pays the UK bill and nothing more.

So from 67 the UK's healthcare advantage is no longer enough. The American pays $328–$510 a month for Medicare Part B, Part D and IRMAA; the UK resident pays $507–$648 a month more in income tax. Healthcare is still the largest single cross-border cost in the model. Once Social Security starts, it stops being the one that decides the order.

Canada and Australia are behind the United States from the first year, and both fall further once Social Security starts. Canada taxes the full 401(k) withdrawal and, under its treaty, 85% of US Social Security; its bill rises from $901 a month at 60 to $1,248 by 80. Australia starts with the lower bill of the two, then taxes Social Security as ordinary income and passes Canada at 67: $1,219 a month against $1,128, rising to $1,351 by 80.

Does it depend on how much you have? Yes.

The UK leads the first phase at every size up to $1.8M, because before 65 the American's marketplace premium is larger than the UK resident's extra tax. At $2.5M the UK's extra tax ($970 a month) is just larger than the American's full-price premium ($949), and the United States leads from the first year.

The finish changes too. From $900k up, the United States finishes first at every size. At $600k the UK stays in front to 90: at that income the UK personal allowance absorbs much of the benefit, so the UK bill after 67 ($320–$398 a month) stays below the American's Medicare ($328–$510).

Retiring later changes who leads at the start

Retire at 60 or 62 and the UK leads the first phase. Retire at 65 or 67, with no marketplace years to pay for, and the United States leads from the start. The last phase is the same every time: United States, UK, Canada, Australia. The spread between best and worst grows from about $290 a month at the start to $507–$703 a month by the eighties, so whatever the choice adds up to on the day you make it, it is about twice that later.

How this was produced

Every figure comes from the same calculation engine that powers the paid AI Retirement Income Planner and the free Retire Abroad comparison tool — not a spreadsheet approximation built for this article. Income is simulated month by month and averaged over each phase.

The generating script refuses to publish numbers it cannot stand behind: it re-derives a documented twenty-value cross-check against the paid planner before writing any output, and asserts the engine's own identity — net income equals gross minus tax minus healthcare — on every phase of every country.

What is modeled

  • US federal income tax — brackets, standard and senior deductions, the taxable-Social-Security calculation, capital gains, NIIT.
  • US healthcare — ACA premiums and the subsidy cliff before 65, then Medicare Part B/D and IRMAA surcharges.
  • UK personal allowance (with its taper) and rates, US Social Security taxed in the UK only (treaty Article 17(3)), and the Foreign Tax Credit.
  • Canadian federal brackets, the basic personal amount as a credit, a provincial rate, and 85% of US Social Security (the treaty exempts 15%).
  • Australian tax-free threshold, marginal rates, the Low Income Tax Offset and the Medicare levy.
  • Required Minimum Distributions from 73, on the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table.
  • Inflation on brackets, deductions, thresholds and healthcare costs, per phase.

What is not — read this before quoting it

  • No healthcare cost outside the US. The UK, Canadian and Australian figures assume the public system covers you at no direct premium. Since healthcare turns out to be the largest single lever here, this assumption is doing a great deal of work. Private cover, an insurance requirement attached to a visa, or a surcharge for new residents would all reduce those columns.
  • US state income tax is set to zero — a no-tax state. That flatters the US column, so any overseas advantage shown is understated.
  • No local social-security entitlements — no CPP, OAS, Age Pension or UK State Pension. This compares the same savings under different tax systems, not different working lives.
  • Nominal dollars, not inflation-adjusted, and no currency risk — everything is priced in USD.
  • Fixed exchange rates. The UK, Canadian and Australian tax thresholds are their 2026 figures converted at fixed rates (£1 = US$1.36, US$1 = C$1.41, US$1 = A$1.41), the same rates the free comparison tool uses. In the paid planner they follow the exchange rate you use, so at other rates those three columns move.
  • Nothing about visas, residency eligibility or domicile. Being allowed to live somewhere is a separate question from what it would cost you.
  • Canada and Australia are modeled with local tax only — no US layer — while the UK column applies both liabilities and the treaty credit. In every phase at every portfolio size here, the Canadian and Australian bills are higher than the US federal figure for the same income, so a US layer would add nothing after the credit. Full explanation on the methodology page.
  • Retirement ages below 60 are excluded. By default the planner holds the 401(k) back before 59½, which opens a phase where almost nothing can be drawn — identical in all four countries, so there is nothing to compare. (The planner can instead withdraw early and price the 10% penalty, or apply the Rule of 55; this study does not.) Bridging that gap needs a taxable account, a Roth ladder or a 72(t), which is a different study.
