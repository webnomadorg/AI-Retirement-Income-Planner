# Promo material — Retirement Plan Guardrails: The Warnings That Keep a Plan Honest

## Facebook Post Snippets


**Snippet 1:** Retirement income has hard edges. One dollar over an ACA threshold can cost thousands in lost cost-sharing help. Withdrawing slightly too little at 75 is a 25% IRS penalty on the shortfall. A Roth conversion at 63 can raise your Medicare premiums at 65. None of those announce themselves in a spreadsheet — which is exactly what guardrails are for.

**Snippet 2:** The most useful warning on a retirement plan is not red. It is amber. Red tells you about a decision you already made. Amber says you are $3,595 below a healthcare threshold and a year-end fund distribution could tip you over — while changing a withdrawal still costs you nothing.

**Snippet 3:** Most retirement projections will happily keep paying you from an account that emptied four years ago. The arithmetic works, the income line never dips, and the plan looks solvent to the last year. A plan worth trusting caps every withdrawal at what the account actually holds — and tells you the age it ran dry.

**Snippet 4:** "Cash runs out at age 69" is not automatically a problem. If cash is your bridge to Social Security, that is the plan working as designed. A guardrail's job is not to tell you that you are wrong. It is to make sure the outcome was a decision rather than an accident.

## Newsletter Summary


Retirement income is full of hard edges — ACA subsidy cliffs, cost-sharing tiers, tax brackets, Social Security taxability, RMD minimums, IRMAA surcharge tiers with a two-year lookback, and accounts quietly reaching zero — and none of them announce themselves in a spreadsheet. This article explains guardrails: the badges and warnings a plan displays because of your own numbers, flagging an edge while it is still free to act. It groups them into four questions: can this plan physically pay (withdrawal shortfalls, account depletion, unfunded one-time expenses, pre-59½ access), am I leaving money on the table (ACA subsidy and cost-sharing bands, dollar headroom to the next threshold, bracket position, the Social Security tax torpedo), is a penalty or surcharge coming (RMD shortfalls, IRMAA and its lookback), and is this really enough (nominal versus real income, income goals measured against the worst phase). It covers how to read them without drowning — learn the color language, scan by family, expect clusters, treat a warning as a question, and accept that some flags are in genuine tension. The closing argument is that guardrails make planning simpler rather than harder: instead of learning every threshold in advance, the workflow becomes change one number, scan for amber, click it, ask why.
