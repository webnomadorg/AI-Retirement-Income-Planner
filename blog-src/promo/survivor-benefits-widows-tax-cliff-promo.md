# Promo material — The Widow's Tax Cliff: Survivor Benefits Explained

## Facebook Post Snippets


**Snippet 1:** When one spouse dies, the household keeps only the larger Social Security check and the survivor moves to single-filer taxes, where the brackets are about half as wide. Income falls, taxes on what remains can rise, and costs barely drop. That is the widow's tax cliff.

**Snippet 2:** The most effective way to protect a surviving spouse is often to delay the higher earner's Social Security. Why? Because the larger benefit is exactly the one the survivor keeps for life. Claiming age is a household decision, not an individual one.

**Snippet 3:** Already a surviving spouse? Your survivor Social Security goes in the Social Security field (so it is taxed correctly), a survivor pension goes in a pension slot, and you plan as a single filer. The income still fits an ordinary retirement plan.

## Newsletter Summary


The widow's tax cliff is one of the largest and least-discussed risks in a married couple's retirement plan. When the first spouse dies, the household keeps only the larger Social Security benefit and the survivor usually moves from married-filing-jointly to single tax brackets, which are roughly half as wide, so income falls while the effective tax rate on what remains can rise. Meanwhile a survivor's costs are typically 70 to 80 percent of a couple's, not half. This article explains the mechanics, the difference between planning ahead as a couple and already being a survivor, and where survivor Social Security and survivor pensions belong in a plan. It also shows how the planner's survivor scenario models the transition (keeping the larger benefit, switching to single filing, continuing pensions at a chosen survivor percentage, and adding a Survivor Income Resilience check), and the main levers to soften the cliff: delaying the higher earner's claim, Roth conversions during the joint years, the pension survivor election, a guaranteed income floor, and life insurance.
