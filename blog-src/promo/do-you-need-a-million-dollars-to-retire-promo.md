# Promo material — Do You Really Need $1 Million to Retire? Find Your Own Number

## Facebook Post Snippets


**Snippet 1:** The "number you need to retire" went from $1.26 million to $1.46 million in twelve months. Nobody's grocery bill moved $200,000 in that time. What moved was how a few thousand survey respondents were feeling in January. That is not a threshold you have failed to clear.

**Snippet 2:** A couple with two average Social Security benefits has around $50,000 a year arriving for life, adjusted for inflation, before touching a cent of savings. If they spend $70,000, their portfolio is being asked to produce $20,000, not $70,000. The million-dollar target assumes your savings does all the work. For most households, it does not.

**Snippet 3:** Two households, one million dollars each. One retires at 66, mortgage paid, Medicare from day one, mostly Roth. The other retires at 58, eleven years left on the mortgage, seven years of health cover to buy, everything in a 401k. Same balance. One is comfortable and one is running a tight plan. No target number tells them apart.

**Snippet 4:** A million dollars in a 401k is not a million dollars. Every withdrawal is ordinary income. The same amount in a Roth behaves completely differently. A target number treats your savings as one pile. Your tax return does not.

**Snippet 5:** Stop asking "do I have enough." Ask "what does what I have actually pay me per month, after tax and healthcare, and does that cover what I actually spend." The first question has no answer. The second one does, and it is yours.

**Snippet 6:** We ran a sample plan on exactly one million dollars. A couple retiring at 62, Social Security at 67, planning through to 90. The sustainable figure came out at about $5,774 a month in today's money, roughly $69,000 a year. The 4% rule on its own would have said $40,000. The difference is the Social Security the rule was never counting.

## Newsletter Summary


The million-dollar retirement target is an average of what people believe they need, not a calculation about any household, and it rose $200,000 in a single year without anyone's circumstances changing by that much. This article explains where the figure comes from, why it cannot know four things that matter more than the balance itself (guaranteed income already arriving, where you live, the healthcare years before 65, and which account types hold the money), and why a balance is not an income. It then turns the question around: average twelve months of real spending, add healthcare and tax, subtract the income you do not have to fund yourself, and what remains is the only part your savings has to cover. The second half covers checking that against reality using the planner's Maximum sustainable spending tool, which searches for the highest withdrawal level a plan supports and lets you choose whether sustainable means staying solvent, surviving randomized market runs, or surviving actual market history. It closes with what to do if the number is short, what to do if you are further ahead than you thought, and a worked contrast of two households holding the same million dollars with very different outcomes.
