# Promo material — Retirement Income: Gross vs Net vs Real (and Reliable vs Flexible)

## Facebook Post Snippets


**Snippet 1:** "$3,000 from your 401(k) plus $2,000 from Social Security" is $5,000 of gross income, but it is not $5,000 you can spend. What matters for your lifestyle is net income, after taxes and healthcare. The monthly number is only the start.

**Snippet 2:** Two retirees, same $4,500 a month, same Social Security. One takes the rest from a traditional IRA, one from a Roth. The gross looks identical; the tax result is not. In retirement, where the income comes from matters as much as how much.

**Snippet 3:** A plan showing $5,000 a month at 62 and $5,500 at 78 looks like a raise, until you adjust for inflation. Nominal income is the dollar amount; real income is what it buys. A plan can look stable in dollars while weakening in real terms.

## Newsletter Summary


A single monthly income target is a useful starting point but a poor stopping point. This article breaks the number into the parts that actually decide whether a plan works: gross versus net income (what comes in versus what is spendable after taxes and healthcare), nominal versus real income (future dollars versus today's purchasing power), and reliable versus flexible income (a guaranteed floor from Social Security and pensions versus withdrawals from your portfolio). The same $4,500 can behave very differently depending on the source and the phase. It also shows how the planner displays gross, net, and real income on every phase card, separates your guaranteed income floor from flexible withdrawals, and checks income adequacy against a floor you set.
