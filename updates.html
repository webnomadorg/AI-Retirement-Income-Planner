Updates & fixes

First released May 2026 · improved continuously ever since

The planner is still being worked on. Tax rules move, edge cases turn up, and buyers write in with things they have spotted. This page lists what has changed lately, and how to pick up the newest version at no extra cost.

Already bought it? Get the update from inside the planner

Open the ? menu in your planner and choose Updates. Type the email address you bought with and your files come straight back — no receipt to dig out, no link you needed to have kept. Your copy also checks on its own, about once a day, and shows an Update available button at the top when there is a newer version.

The planner is a one-time purchase. There is no upgrade fee and no subscription; you simply download the newer file and open it in place of the old one.

On an older copy, or moving your plan across

Bought before this existed, so your planner has no Updates tab? Email Email us from the address you bought with and we will send your files back — a real person reads that inbox. The thank-you page you were sent after your purchase also still works.

Your saved work is not affected by updating. Export your plan as a JSON file from the old copy, open the new one, and import it — every figure comes across. Plans made in an earlier version load in a later one.

  • v392

    ACA premium figures corrected, and a refresh now shows its working · 27 August

    If your plan has ACA years before Medicare, your figures will move slightly with this update, and not in your favor. Two separate things were wrong with how those years were priced, and both pushed the same way.

    The percentages were rounded. The ACA caps your premium at a share of your income that climbs as your income does. The planner was carrying those figures as 3%, 4%, 6% and 8% where the published 2026 schedule is 3.14%, 4.19%, 6.60% and 8.44%.

    And the climb was being treated as a staircase. The real table rises steadily across each income band; the planner applied one flat rate for the whole band, the rate from its lower edge. So unless your income sat right at the bottom of a band, your premium came out low — by a little at the start of a band and by more towards the top of it. The planner now follows the published curve between the same published points, which is what it should have been doing all along. Small differences on their own, but they apply to every ACA month in the plan, and understating a cost quietly overstates what you have left to spend.

    The help text was out of date in a way that mattered more. It told you the ACA caps your premium at 8.5% of your income. That was the enhanced-subsidy cap, and it expired at the end of last year alongside the return of the 400% cliff. Five places in the planner said it. All five now describe the real sliding scale.

    Those percentages can be refreshed now, like every other tax figure. They had been written into the calculation itself rather than stored as settings, which meant Fetch current rates could not reach them — they were only correctable by me, in a release, which is how they came to drift without anyone noticing. They are ordinary figures on the Edit values tab now, and the annual refresh picks them up with the rest.

    After a refresh, each figure now tells you what happened to it. The old summary said how many figures were updated and stopped there, which left the more useful question unanswered: what about all the others? Every figure is now marked — updated, confirmed as already correct, changed but not applied, or requested and not returned. That last one is the case worth catching: a figure the lookup quietly skipped used to look exactly like one it had checked and found fine. A control on the Edit values tab quietens the markers once you have read them, though the count of anything unchecked stays visible either way.

    Smaller thing in the same release: the blue dot marking a value you have changed since your baseline was rendering red in one of the color themes, which read as an error rather than a note to yourself. It is blue everywhere again.

    Housekeeping: the planner now tells me which version it is · 24 August

    Nothing works differently and none of your figures will change.

    Here is what changed and why. When I release an update I have had no way of knowing whether it reaches anyone — whether people are still running a version from months ago, or whether the update notice works at all. So once a day the planner now sends the site a single number: the version it is running. That is the whole message.     Canadian tax figures corrected, and a safer rate review · 22 August

    If you plan in Canadian dollars, your figures will shift slightly with this update. The planner was carrying a mixture of two tax years: the lowest federal rate and the first two bracket ceilings from one year, the upper two ceilings from another. That combination matched no actual year. The federal rates and all four ceilings, plus the basic personal amount, are now a single current set. The 2026 Medicare Part D premium is updated too.

    The rate review now shows you the whole IRMAA table. That ladder holds twenty separate figures — five income thresholds and both a Part B and a Part D surcharge for each — and the review used to summarize it in a single line. You could not see which of the twenty had actually moved, which is not really a review at all. It now lays out every tier side by side, marking only what changed.

    It will also tell you when a proposed change is not a real one. If every figure comes back within a dollar of what you already have, that is a rounding artefact rather than a rate change — Medicare does not adjust a surcharge by pennies — and the planner says so instead of quietly offering to overwrite correct numbers with slightly wrong ones.

  • v365

    A correction for anyone retiring to the UK, and this year’s tax figures built in · 22 August

    If you are planning a move to the UK, your numbers will change with this update, and not in your favor. The planner treated US Social Security as taxable only in the United States. That is the wrong way round. Article 17(3) of the US–UK tax treaty says social security is taxable only in the country you live in — so once you are a UK resident, your Social Security is taxed by the UK and not by the US. It is also a “saved exception”, which means it still applies even though you are a US citizen.

    The practical effect is that UK-resident plans now show a higher tax bill and less net income. Depending on the size of your benefit that can be a few thousand dollars a year. Nobody enjoys an update that makes the picture worse, but a plan built on the wrong rule is not worth having, and it is better to find this now than at your first UK tax return. The Foreign Tax Credit does not cancel it out: the UK starts taxing at 20% above the personal allowance, while the US taxed only part of the benefit behind a standard deduction.

    The tax figures the planner starts with are current again. A fresh download now opens with this year’s numbers already in place — the standard deduction, the tax bands, the poverty levels behind ACA subsidies, the Medicare Part B and Part D premiums, and the full six-bracket IRMAA table. Previously you had to refresh those yourself before the plan meant much. You still can, and should, once a new year’s figures are announced.

    Long-run assumptions are now kept separate from this year’s facts. Inflation, the Social Security COLA and the UK triple lock are not figures to be looked up and applied for ever — the planner compounds them across your whole retirement, so one year’s announcement is not the rate to assume for the next thirty. Refreshing rates no longer touches them, and if the AI does suggest a change to one, it now arrives unticked with an explanation of why it matters.

    The interactive demo on this site has been rebuilt on the same figures, so what you try before buying matches what you get.

    Fetch current rates now covers everything — and shows you the figures before it uses them · 22 August

    The button at the bottom of the Edit tab is called Fetch current rates now, because tax brackets were only ever half of what it does. It reaches every annual figure in the planner: the whole six-bracket IRMAA ladder rather than just the first threshold, the Part B and Part D premiums, the poverty levels behind your ACA subsidy, the RMD age, the UK State Pension age, the disability earnings limits, and the Australian marginal rates.

    The bigger change is what happens when the answer comes back. It used to go straight into your plan. Now you get a table first: every figure it wants to change, what you have beside what it proposes, and a tick box against each one. Nothing moves until you press Apply.

    Anything that looks wrong arrives already unticked, with the reason next to it — a figure outside the range that number can sensibly take, brackets that do not rise in order, a value that has jumped much further than a year's indexation would explain. These figures come from an AI, and an AI states a wrong number in exactly the same tone as a right one. Putting them in front of you first is the only honest way to hand them over.

    Two things make the answers better as well as safer. Where your AI provider supports it, the lookup now searches the web rather than answering from memory — which matters, because every number here is reset each year and a model working from memory will give you last year's. And it only asks about the figures your plan actually uses, so a US plan is not paying for a lookup of Canadian brackets. There is a tick box if you want the lot.

    Under the button you will now see when the figures were last refreshed, so a plan carrying last year's numbers says so instead of looking exactly like one you updated this morning.

    Australian plans: the four marginal rates are now proper settings you can see and edit, alongside the bracket ceilings and the Medicare levy threshold, and they arrive set to the current post-Stage-3 rates. If you plan in Australian dollars, your tax figures will shift a little to match.

That is not a bargain — it is the point where the conversion has emptied the 401k, so there is less left to withdraw and be taxed on.   • v315

    There are two Roth 5-year rules, and the Glossary now explains both · 19 August

    Almost everyone merges these into one rule, and mixing them up is how a conversion ladder goes wrong. One clock runs once, from your first ever Roth contribution, and decides whether your earnings come out tax-free. A completely separate clock runs on every single conversion and decides whether that converted money escapes the 10% penalty before 59½.

    The new The two Roth 5-year rules entry in the Glossary lays out both, plus the bit that makes a ladder work at all: the order money leaves a Roth — your contributions first, then conversions oldest-first, then earnings. There is also a quirk worth knowing, that both clocks run on tax years rather than birthdays, so a conversion made in December seasons after about four years and one month.

  • v314

    A warning if your Roth conversion ladder is spent too soon · 19 August

    A conversion ladder is one of the standard ways to bridge to 59½, and there is a rule inside it that is easy to miss: each conversion has to sit for five years before you can spend it penalty-free, and every conversion starts its own separate clock. Your own contributions come out at any age, which is what makes a Roth feel like an open account — but converted money spent too early carries the same 10% penalty you were trying to avoid.

    The planner now watches that for you. A Roth Conversion Seasoning check in Plan health works out how much of what you are spending has genuinely had five years to season, and tells you the age at which your plan runs past it, and by how much — so a ladder that converts at 50 and spends at 52 shows its cost rather than reading as free.

    It only appears if your plan has both conversions and a Roth withdrawal before 59½; otherwise you will not see it. And it is a flag rather than a tax figure: the planner holds your Roth as one balance rather than tracking each conversion separately, so it can tell you the pattern looks wrong but not compute the bill. That one is worth a conversation with a tax professional.

  • v312

    Retiring before 59½? You can now take the 401k early and see what it costs · 18 August

    Until now, if you retired before 59½ the planner simply held your 401k back and made you fund those years from everything else. That is one real strategy, but it is not the only one — you can take money out early, you just pay a 10% penalty on top of the usual income tax. Holding it back meant some workable early retirements could look out of reach when they were simply expensive, with no way to tell which.

    The Pre 59½ phase now asks how you are reaching the account. Leave it untouched as before, or withdraw and have the 10% priced in — it shows as its own line rather than being folded into your tax figure, so you can see exactly what the choice costs, in the plan and in the PDF report.

    If you stopped work at 55 or later there is also the Rule of 55, which removes that penalty entirely from the plan of the employer you just left. It is easy to lose by accident — rolling the money into an IRA destroys it, and leaving at 50 and waiting until 55 does not count — so the planner spells out the conditions rather than just offering a tickbox. Nothing changes unless you change it: existing plans still open exactly as you left them.

    A 72(t)/SEPP series remains outside what the planner models, and it now says so where that matters. If you are retiring well before 55, that is the route worth discussing with a tax professional.

    Retired or stopped work? Medicare may not have to use your old income · 6 August

    Medicare sets its high-income surcharge from your tax return two years ago. That is fine while your income is steady, and well wide of the mark the year after it falls away — the year you retire, or the year you stop work because of illness. You can be charged a high-income surcharge out of an income you no longer have.

    Social Security knows this. Form SSA-44 lets you ask them to use your current income instead, after a life-changing event: retiring or cutting your hours, the death of a spouse, marriage or divorce, losing a pension. The planner now lets you model that — or simply type the premium your SSA notice actually quotes, if you already have one.

    We have been careful with the wording, because this is an assumption rather than a promise: Social Security decides each request on its merits and looks at it again every year. The planner says so wherever you use it. On a test plan with SSDI it is worth $733 a month, which is well worth knowing you may be able to claim back.

    IRMAA has six brackets, not one · 6 August

    The Medicare high-income surcharge arrived in the planner as a single figure — one bracket, one amount. There are in fact six, and the range is wide: about $95 a month at the bottom against about $640 at the top. If you are running Roth conversions of any size, selling a business, or taking a big year of withdrawals, you land somewhere in the middle of that table, so a single figure was only ever right at the foot of it.

    All six brackets are now in the planner, with Part B and Part D listed separately because they are separate bills. The bracket amounts sit on the Edit tab if you want to check them against your own notice, and Fetch current rates refreshes them when CMS resets them each autumn.

  • v280

    Tax-free bond interest can still raise your tax bill · 6 August

    You can now enter tax-exempt interest — municipal bonds — phase by phase, next to rental income. It is not taxed, and the planner does not tax it.

    But it is not invisible either, and this is the part that catches people out. That interest counts toward the income test that decides how much of your Social Security is taxable. On a test plan, adding $30,000 of tax-free bond interest took the tax bill from nothing to $1,645 a year — not by taxing the bonds, but by dragging the taxable share of Social Security from 8% up to 85%. Buying municipal bonds to reduce a tax bill can increase it, and entering the interest is what makes that visible.

    It also counts toward the income figures behind your ACA subsidy and your IRMAA bracket, so putting it in makes those warnings honest.

    The IRMAA check now reads the right MAGI · 6 August

    “MAGI” is not one number. The health insurance marketplace counts your Social Security in full; Medicare counts only the part that is actually taxable, because it reads your tax return. The planner had one figure doing both jobs; the two are now calculated separately.

    That matters most for anyone living mainly on Social Security or SSDI, whose benefit is barely taxable. On one test plan the figure Medicare should read is $1,675 rather than $32,462 — the difference between a surcharge applying in every Medicare year and not applying at all. Both figures are now shown separately under Under the hood, so you can see which is which.

    Better handling for plans where Medicare starts mid-phase · 6 August

    If you're on SSDI, the planner now fits your situation · 30 July

    Following on from the disability update below. If you are receiving SSDI — Social Security Disability — the planner was built around the rules for a retirement benefit, and those are not the rules for SSDI. Two things follow from that, and both were understating your position on paper.
