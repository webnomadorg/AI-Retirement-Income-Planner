Updates & fixes

First released May 2026 · improved continuously ever since

The planner is still being worked on. Tax rules move, edge cases turn up, and buyers write in with things they have spotted. This page lists what has changed lately, and how to pick up the newest version at no extra cost.

Already bought it? Get the update from inside the planner

Open the ? menu in your planner and choose Updates. Type the email address you bought with and your files come straight back — no receipt to dig out, no link you needed to have kept. Your copy also checks on its own, about once a day, and shows an Update available button at the top when there is a newer version.

The planner is a one-time purchase. There is no upgrade fee and no subscription; you simply download the newer file and open it in place of the old one.

On an older copy, or moving your plan across

Bought before this existed, so your planner has no Updates tab? Email dev@webnomad.org from the address you bought with and we will send your files back — a real person reads that inbox. The thank-you page you were sent after your purchase also still works.

Your saved work is not affected by updating. Export your plan as a JSON file from the old copy, open the new one, and import it — every figure comes across. Plans made in an earlier version load in a later one.

    See where you stand on the two cliffs that cost the most · 19 August

    Two of the most expensive edges in US retirement are step changes rather than gradual slopes: cross the ACA subsidy cliff by a single dollar and the whole subsidy goes, and the IRMAA surcharge jumps in tiers based on your income from two years earlier. The planner has always calculated both and explained them in words.     And the account balances chart on the Balance tab now switches between By phase and By year. A plan can look perfectly healthy at every phase boundary and still dip hard somewhere inside one — the yearly view shows that.

  • v327

    Four new charts — and three things they caught · 19 August

    The stress test is the one I think you will notice first. Those twelve scenarios are now colour-shaded by how the portfolio ends up, so instead of reading twelve boxes of numbers you can see the shape of where your plan gets thin. The SS Optimizer finally draws the picture that tab has always been about: total benefit received by age, one line for each claiming age, and the point where waiting catches up is simply where the lines cross. The Balance tab shows which tax bands each phase of your income actually lands in. And the Annuities tab plots annuity income against what the premium would have grown to if you had just invested it.

    Drawing things has a way of exposing what tables hide, and three problems turned up while I was building these.

    The Canadian and Australian tax charts were wrong, and by a lot — about $57,000 in one test plan. Canada's provincial tax and Australia's Medicare Levy are flat charges on your whole income rather than tax bands, and they were being counted as if they were an extra band on top. That is now fixed, and the chart says plainly that those levies sit outside the bands it draws.

    The Annuities tab was overstating the case for annuities. It said an annuity "wins" once its income overtakes what investing would have earned, and marked years "Ahead" from break-even. But break-even there only ever meant the annuity had paid back the premium — a much lower bar. On a typical setup the income never catches investing at all: $492,000 paid out against $2.1 million invested. The wording now says what is actually being measured, and the chart lets you see it for yourself.

    The annuity timeline table was showing a single row for most people. If your purchase age landed on a half year — 59½, which is the common case — it quietly filtered out almost everything. It shows the full timeline now.

    One smaller fix that has been bothering me for a while: the charts used to flicker and re-animate every time you typed in a field. They hold still now, and animate when you actually change view.

  • v318

    See which pot is paying for each year of your retirement · 19 August

    The Balance tab could already tell you, year by year, what came out of each account and what your pensions paid — but only as a table. Reading a plan that way means holding thirty rows of numbers in your head at once, and the shape of the thing never really appears.

    There is now a chart above that table, and it is driven by the same four switches you were already using. Every bar is one year. Each colour is one source: your 401k, cash and equity on the Account view, and on the Income view your Social Security, state and workplace pensions, part-time work and rental income stacked on top. Hover any bar and it lists every source for that year with the total. Switch to By phase and each bar becomes a typical year in that phase instead.

    What it makes obvious in a second is the handover — the point where you stop living off savings and your pensions take over, and whether the bar dips in between. The colours follow whatever palette you have chosen under Appearance, so it matches the rest of your plan.

    One thing worth saying plainly: these bars are your gross income, before tax and healthcare. Those are worked out once per phase rather than per year, so they stay in the table underneath where they can be labelled honestly, instead of being divided into years here and dressed up as precision they do not have.

    While I was in there I fixed something small that had been irritating me: those four buttons shifted sideways slightly every time you clicked one. They hold still now.

    Your PDF report now adds up · 19 August

  • v315

    There are two Roth 5-year rules, and the Glossary now explains both · 19 August

    Almost everyone merges these into one rule, and mixing them up is how a conversion ladder goes wrong. One clock runs once, from your first ever Roth contribution, and decides whether your earnings come out tax-free. A completely separate clock runs on every single conversion and decides whether that converted money escapes the 10% penalty before 59½.

    The new The two Roth 5-year rules entry in the Glossary lays out both, plus the bit that makes a ladder work at all: the order money leaves a Roth — your contributions first, then conversions oldest-first, then earnings. There is also a quirk worth knowing, that both clocks run on tax years rather than birthdays, so a conversion made in December seasons after about four years and one month.

  • v314

    A warning if your Roth conversion ladder is spent too soon · 19 August

    A conversion ladder is one of the standard ways to bridge to 59½, and there is a rule inside it that is easy to miss: each conversion has to sit for five years before you can spend it penalty-free, and every conversion starts its own separate clock. Your own contributions come out at any age, which is what makes a Roth feel like an open account — but converted money spent too early carries the same 10% penalty you were trying to avoid.

    The planner was not telling you that. You could convert at 50 and spend it at 52 and it reported no cost at all. There is now a Roth Conversion Seasoning check in Plan health: it works out how much of what you are spending has actually had five years to season, and tells you the age at which your plan runs past it, and by how much.

    It only appears if your plan has both conversions and a Roth withdrawal before 59½ — otherwise it stays out of the way. And it is a flag rather than a tax figure: the planner holds your Roth as one balance rather than tracking each conversion separately, so it can tell you the pattern looks wrong but not compute the bill. That one is worth a conversation with a tax professional.

  • v312

    Retiring before 59½? You can now take the 401k early and see what it costs · 18 August

    Until now, if you retired before 59½ the planner simply held your 401k back and made you fund those years from everything else. That is one real strategy, but it is not the only one — you can take money out early, you just pay a 10% penalty on top of the usual income tax. Locking it meant some perfectly workable early retirements looked impossible when they were only expensive, and you had no way to find out which.

    The Pre 59½ phase now asks how you are reaching the account. Leave it untouched as before, or withdraw and have the 10% priced in — it shows as its own line rather than quietly swelling your tax figure, so you can see exactly what the choice costs, in the plan and in the PDF report.

    If you stopped work at 55 or later there is also the Rule of 55, which removes that penalty entirely from the plan of the employer you just left. It is easy to lose by accident — rolling the money into an IRA destroys it, and leaving at 50 and waiting until 55 does not count — so the planner spells out the conditions rather than just offering a tickbox. Nothing changes unless you change it: existing plans still open exactly as you left them.

    One thing still not modelled, and the planner now says so where it matters: a 72(t)/SEPP series. If you are retiring well before 55, that is the route worth discussing with a tax professional.

  • v292

    Retired or stopped work? Medicare may not have to use your old income · 6 August

    Medicare sets its high-income surcharge from your tax return two years ago. That is fine while your income is steady, and badly wrong the year after it falls away — the year you retire, or the year you stop work because of illness. You can be charged a high-income surcharge out of an income you no longer have.

    Social Security knows this. Form SSA-44 lets you ask them to use your current income instead, after a life-changing event: retiring or cutting your hours, the death of a spouse, marriage or divorce, losing a pension. The planner now lets you model that — or simply type the premium your SSA notice actually quotes, if you already have one.

    We have been careful with the wording, because this is an assumption rather than a promise: Social Security decides each request on its merits and looks at it again every year. The planner says so wherever you use it. On a test plan with SSDI it is worth $733 a month, which is the sort of figure worth knowing you might be able to claim back.

  • v285

    IRMAA has six brackets, not one · 6 August

    The Medicare high-income surcharge came in as a single figure — one bracket, one amount. In reality there are six, and the gap between the bottom and the top is enormous: about $95 a month against about $640. If you are running Roth conversions of any size, or selling a business, or taking a big year of withdrawals, you land in the middle of that table, and we were quoting you the bottom of it.

    All six brackets are now in the planner, with Part B and Part D listed separately because they are separate bills. You can see which bracket a phase lands in, and what each part costs, under Under the hood. The bracket amounts sit on the Edit tab if you want to check them against your own notice, and Fetch current rates refreshes them when CMS resets them each autumn.

    One detail worth knowing because it works against you slowly: the top bracket is fixed in law rather than rising with inflation, so it catches more people every year. The planner models it that way rather than pretending it drifts upward.

  • v280

    Tax-free bond interest can still raise your tax bill · 6 August

    You can now enter tax-exempt interest — municipal bonds — phase by phase, next to rental income. It is not taxed, and the planner does not tax it.

    But it is not invisible either, and this is the part that catches people out. That interest counts toward the income test that decides how much of your Social Security is taxable. On a test plan, adding $30,000 of tax-free bond interest took the tax bill from nothing to $1,645 a year — not by taxing the bonds, but by dragging the taxable share of Social Security from 8% up to 85%. Buying municipal bonds to reduce a tax bill can increase it. Leaving the interest out of your plan hid that entirely.

    It also counts toward the income figures behind your ACA subsidy and your IRMAA bracket, so putting it in makes those warnings honest.

  • v280

    The IRMAA check was reading the wrong income figure · 6 August

    “MAGI” is not one number. The health insurance marketplace counts your Social Security in full; Medicare counts only the part that is actually taxable, because it reads your tax return. The planner had one figure doing both jobs, and used the larger one for Medicare.

    That overstated the income IRMAA sees, and it hurt exactly the people it should not have: anyone living mainly on Social Security or SSDI, whose benefit is barely taxable. On one test plan the figure being checked was $32,462 when the real one was $1,675 — enough to charge a surcharge in every Medicare year that should never have applied. Both figures are now shown separately under Under the hood, so you can see which is which.

  • v274

    Correcting what we said about capital gains · 2 August

    A customer asked how the planner handles capital gains when you are living off a brokerage account in early retirement. Checking the answer properly, we found the help text was wrong — in three places it said equity gains were not treated as ordinary income and got a lower rate. The calculations have never worked that way, and the help text has now been corrected to match what the planner actually does.

    What it actually does: when the plan sells from your taxable account, only the gain inside that sale is taxed — getting your own money back is not income. The split comes from the Pre-plan taxable gain % on the Edit tab, so it is worth setting that from your broker's cost-basis report; left at zero, the planner assumes there is no gain to tax.

    That gain is then taxed at ordinary income rates. Real long-term gains usually qualify for the lower 0% / 15% / 20% rates, so the planner shows more capital-gains tax than you would actually pay, never less. We would rather err that way, but you should know it is there — the gap is widest if your income is low enough that your real rate would be 0%, which is common when you are bridging to Medicare on taxable savings. It is written up in full under Under the hood, alongside the note about tax being worked out per phase.

    The part that usually matters more was already right: gains count in full toward your MAGI, so they drive ACA subsidies, the Silver cost-sharing band, the subsidy cliff and the Medicare IRMAA lookback exactly as they would in reality.

    See your plan year by year — by account, or by income · 2 August

    The planner has always shown you a phase at a time: five or six cards, each covering a stretch of years. That is the right way to design a plan, but it is an awkward way to answer questions like “what is left in the Roth when I am 78?” or “which year does the cash actually run out?”

    There is now a table at the bottom of the Balance tab with two switches. Account view gives you a column per account and shows the balance each one ends on, with the money that moved through it underneath — what you drew, what was paid in, anything converted, and how much it grew. Income view turns it round to show where the money came from: what you took from the portfolio, your Social Security, other pensions, any work or rental income, then what tax and healthcare took and what was left.

    The second switch sets the rows. By phase gives you one line per phase. By year opens it out to a line per year, which is where it earns its keep: you can watch balances compound, see the exact year a pension starts and what it is worth by then, and spot the year an account empties. Where a withdrawal was more than the account could actually supply, the table says so rather than quietly showing the figure you asked for.

    Download CSV saves whatever you are looking at, ready to open in a spreadsheet or hand to your accountant.

    One thing we want to be straight about, and you will find it written up in full under Under the hood. The planner works out tax once per phase, on that phase's income, rather than separately for each year. When your income holds steady through a phase those are the same number exactly. When it moves around inside a phase — a conversion ladder running for a few years, or an account emptying partway through — the phase figure comes out slightly low, never high. We have now put numbers on it: nothing at all if your income stays inside one tax band, around 5–6% of the tax if it drifts up through one, and up to about 20% in the lumpiest cases. That is why the year-by-year table shows tax as a phase figure spanning its years instead of inventing one for each year. We would rather tell you where the estimate is soft than let it look more precise than it is.

    Also tidied: the Downloads list in the ? menu now pulls the user guide, the free eBook and the technical documents straight from our own website rather than a file-sharing link. It means you always get the current version — we were keeping two copies of each and they had started to drift apart. Two documents that were on the website but never listed in the planner have been added while we were in there: Methodology & Assumptions and Calculation Validation.

    Two smaller things. If you use Fit all phases to get every card on one row, the Nominal and Real income figures were running past the edge of their boxes and the last card was cutting off mid-number — they now sit one above the other and fit properly. And the disclaimer has been expanded to cover the things it should have all along, including that AI answers can be wrong about tax figures and need checking.

    On the same note: there are now proper Software Terms on the website, readable before you buy rather than only inside the file afterwards. They set out the licence, what the planner does and does not promise, refunds, and which law applies — and they name the person you are actually buying from, Paul Hankin, trading as WebNomad Studio. Nothing about what you have bought has changed; it is simply written down properly now.

    Two rough edges on the SSDI update, smoothed · 30 July

    The SSDI update below shipped with two corners cut, and neither needed to stay cut.

It now splits the cost properly: 2½ of those 4½ years on Medicare, the rest on marketplace cover, priced accordingly, with the subsidy still counted for the months before Medicare starts. The phase card, the PDF and the AI all label it ACA → Medicare so you can see what is happening rather than wondering why the figure looks odd.

    If you are not on SSDI, none of this touches your plan — we checked every one of our test scenarios comes out to the penny it did before.

    If you're on SSDI, the planner now fits your situation · 30 July

    Following on from the disability update below. If you are receiving SSDI — Social Security Disability — the planner was quietly making you fit a mould built for people claiming a retirement benefit, and it did not fit. Two things were wrong, and both cost you money on paper.

    First, it would not let you enter a start age below 62. That rule exists because you cannot claim a retirement benefit earlier than that. SSDI has no such limit, and it does not get the reduction you take for claiming early — it pays your full retirement-age amount from the day it starts. So if your SSDI began at 55, the planner simply would not show that income. Tick the new I'm receiving SSDI box and the limit lifts: put in the age it actually started and your income appears from there. Nothing else changes, because SSDI already gets the same annual increase and the same tax treatment as Social Security — and at full retirement age it just becomes your retirement benefit at the same figure.

    Second, and this one matters more: SSDI brings Medicare 24 months after your benefit starts, whatever your age. Someone on SSDI at 55 usually has Medicare at 57. The planner assumed 65 for everybody, so it had you on marketplace insurance with subsidies you would not have been entitled to, and left out Medicare premiums for years. Ticking the box asks when your Medicare began and models it properly from then.

    Worth saying plainly: employer, private and VA disability are not SSDI, and still belong in the pension and disability fields, where you set their own annual increase and the age they stop.

  • v239

    Disability that stops at 65 · 30 July

    A customer got in touch about disability income, and it turned up something worth fixing. Most employer and private long-term disability policies stop paying at 65, or at Social Security full retirement age — that is the point the cover is designed to hand over to your retirement income. The planner had no way to tell it that, so it kept paying the benefit for the rest of your life, on top of your Social Security. On a $3,000 a month benefit that is around $900,000 of income you were never going to get, and it made the plan look better than it is.

    Both pension and disability streams now have an end age. Leave it blank and nothing changes — a normal pension, an annuity or VA disability carries on for life exactly as before, and every plan you have already saved is untouched. Put 65 in it and the benefit stops there. If it stops before your Social Security starts, you will see the gap on the phase cards, which is the whole point of putting it in.

    The question behind all this was whether disability should rise every year the way Social Security does. It should not, and it already did not have to — each pension and disability stream has always had its own annual increase, separate from the Social Security one, and setting it to 0 keeps the payment flat for life. That setting lives on the Inflation card, which is why it was easy to miss, so the disability field now says so where you are typing.

    One thing worth checking if it applies to you: SSDI belongs in the Social Security box, not the disability box. It rises with the Social Security increase, it turns into your retirement benefit automatically at full retirement age at the same amount, and it is taxed under Social Security rules — so putting it in the disability field gets the tax wrong. Employer, private and VA disability all belong in the disability fields.

  • v236

    The UK State Pension now has one off switch · 30 July

    A customer wrote in to say they could not work out how to stop the UK State Pension appearing in their later phases, and they were right — it was our fault, not theirs. The amount sat in two places: in Income streams, where you would expect it, and again in a box on the Phase 4 and Phase 5 cards. The phase box was the one the sums actually used, so setting it to zero in one place changed that phase and left the others still paying it, while the Income streams box carried on showing the old figure. There was no way to tell which one was in charge.

    There is now one figure, in Income streams, and that is the only place it is set. If the UK State Pension does not apply to you, put a 0 there and it is gone from every phase. The boxes on the phase cards are still there so you can see what each phase receives, but they are read-only and say where to go to change them. Social Security works the same way now, for the same reason.

    Two things this quietly fixes. If you claim Social Security part-way through a phase, the planner splits that phase in two — and the second half was being paid neither your Social Security nor your UK State Pension, which pulled that stretch of your plan down for no reason. And if you are modelling what happens when one of you dies, the survivor was being shown the original benefit rather than the larger one they actually inherit, so their income was understated. Both are right now. Open your plan and check the phases after 67 look the way you expect.

  • Website

    A free way to compare retiring in four countries · 29 July

    If you have ever wondered what your savings would actually be worth living in Britain, Canada or Australia instead of the States, there is now a free page on the site that shows you: the Retire Abroad comparison. Put your balances, your monthly withdrawals and your Social Security in once, and it lays out what you would have left each month in all four countries side by side, after income tax and after healthcare.

    It runs the same sums as the planner you bought, so the two agree — we checked every figure against the current build before putting it up. What surprised us with the numbers we tried is that the order changes as you get older: the country that leaves you best off at 60 is not the one that leaves you best off at 80, because Medicare at 65, Social Security and the withdrawals you are forced to take from 73 all arrive at different moments and pull in different directions.

    It is free and there is nothing to sign up for. It only handles one straightforward scenario — no phases, no saved comparisons — so it is a taster rather than a replacement for the planner. Do send it to anyone you know who is weighing up a move.

  • v230

    How close a one-off takes you to the line · 27 July

    Following on from the update below: paying for something out of a 401k and paying for it by selling investments are not the same thing. The whole 401k withdrawal counts as income that year, but on a sale you are only taxed on the growth — you already paid tax on the money you put in. The same $200,000 adds $200,000 to your income from a 401k but nearer $60,000 from a brokerage account, which is worth knowing before you decide where the money comes from. It also means a sale often lands just under the ACA or Medicare line rather than over it, and until now nothing told you it had moved you at all. The ACA and Medicare panels now say where you end up either way: over the line, with the warning you'd expect, or under it with the amount you have left — "still under the $66,806 cliff, with $6,465 to spare" — and a reminder that anything else arriving that year, a fund payout or a bit of interest, eats into what's left.

  • v229

    A warning where you'd actually look for it · 27 July

    If you pay for something large out of a 401k, that withdrawal counts as income in the year you take it — enough, often, to put you over the ACA subsidy cliff or into a higher Medicare band. The planner works in multi-year phases, so the MAGI figure it shows you is the average across the phase, and a single big year barely moves it. That meant you could click the ACA subsidy eligible badge and be told everything was fine, in a year the subsidy would have gone. The planner already flagged this on the phase card, but not in the panel you open to check. Now it does: the ACA and Medicare panels show the phase average as before, then tell you plainly what your income would be in the single year you take the expense, which line it crosses, and what it would cost you — along with the usual ways round it, like paying from cash or Roth savings, or splitting it across two years.

  • v228

    Every one-off expense shown separately in the working · 27 July

    If you put more than one cash event in the same phase, Under the hood only appeared to list the first. The figures were right — it was adding events together by account, so two expenses both paid from cash showed up as a single line, and the second one looked like it had gone missing. Each event is now listed on its own, by the name you gave it, showing what it took from each account, the tax on it, and how much reached the expense. Where a phase has several, the combined totals are shown underneath so everything still adds up. The same applies to the calculations pages in your printed report.

  • v218–v227

    You choose which account pays for a one-off expense · 27 July

    Add a new roof or a car on the Lump sums tab and there is now a Paid from box next to it. Pick Cash and the planner takes it from cash and nothing else — if there isn't enough, it tells you how much is short instead of quietly selling your investments or dipping into your 401k to cover it. That was the old behaviour, and it meant a plan could look fine while it had actually raided a retirement account to pay the builder. If you'd rather it did spill over, tick Use other accounts if short and pick exactly which accounts it may use — you might be happy for it to come out of the brokerage account but want the Roth left alone. There's also a Custom split if you want to say "$20,000 from cash, $40,000 from the brokerage account", and it shows you what each account will actually hold at that point in your plan. Money coming in works the same way: an inheritance or a house sale can be paid straight into your brokerage account instead of sitting in cash for twenty years earning the cash rate.

  • v218–v227

    What that expense really costs, before you commit to it · 27 July

    This one changes some numbers, so it's worth reading. If you take money out of a 401k to pay for something, that withdrawal is taxable income, and if you sell investments to pay for it you owe tax on the growth. We weren't charging that. A $60,000 kitchen paid from a 401k came out of the planner costing exactly $60,000, when in reality the taxman takes a slice first and roughly $45,000 of it reaches the kitchen. Now you see both figures. Under each event on the Lump sums tab is a line telling you what leaves each account, what goes to tax, and what actually reaches the expense — so you can weigh it up there and then, and try paying for it a different way if you'd rather. If you want the full amount to get there, tick Withdraw extra to cover the tax and the planner works out the larger withdrawal for you: for that kitchen, $69,477 out of the 401k so the full $60,000 arrives. It will also warn you if a withdrawal that size would push you over the ACA subsidy cliff or into a higher Medicare band in the year you take it. Your account balances are unchanged — that money was always leaving the account. What's changed is that you can now see how much of it never reached what you were buying, so an expense funded from a retirement account will look dearer than it did before. That's the honest figure, and it's the one worth planning against.

    Your total tax figure now explains itself · 26 July

Like the other calculation panels, it links straight through to the full working under the hood. Phase 5 has also been given its own colour: its badge and Phase 3's were near-identical shades of amber, which made two very different parts of your plan look alike at a glance.

    Under the hood: every calculation, shown in full · 26 July

The longer view

How the planner has grown

Versions 1 to 5 are still sold as simpler, cheaper tools for people who do not need the full picture. Versions 6 and 7 build on them. Each one is a single file you own outright.

Version 1 — the core planner

Month-by-month retirement income across life phases: 401(k), cash and equities, Social Security, UK State Pension, Medicare, ACA costs, tax estimates and charts, for a single filer.

Version 2 — Roth and couples

Married filing jointly, a spouse's Social Security, Roth balances, withdrawals and conversions, plus the Social Security Optimizer for comparing claiming ages.

Version 3 — rental income and RMDs

Rental and other passive income, and estimates of the required minimum distributions that begin in your seventies.

Version 4 — Canada, Australia and custom phases

CPP and OAS, Superannuation and the Age Pension, plus the ability to set your own phase boundary ages instead of the US defaults.

Version 5 — the complete planner

Separate Social Security claiming ages, stress testing, six drawdown strategies (the 4% rule, variable percentage, three buckets, floor & upside, guardrails and Monte Carlo) and Plan Health confidence scoring.

A built-in AI advisor that reads your plan, one-click tax-rate updates, an annuity calculator, and a whole educational layer: explainers beside the confusing fields, a glossary, video tutorials and a "concepts to learn" panel.

Version 7 — sharper, broader, more accessible

The current release. More accurate Social Security taxation using the IRS provisional-income method, Net Investment Income Tax, the IRMAA two-year lookback, optional state income tax, a Full Retirement Age set from your birth year, separate healthcare inflation, inflation-aware Monte Carlo, survivor modelling with coordinated couples' claiming, the Plan-with-AI co-pilot and "Ask AI about this" buttons throughout — plus a high-contrast theme and larger text options.
